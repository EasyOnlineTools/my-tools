How to Calculate Compound Interest: Formula, Examples & Easy Guide

Compound interest is one of the most important concepts in personal finance, savings and investing. Unlike simple interest, compound interest allows previously earned interest to become part of the balance and earn additional interest over time.

In this guide, you will learn how to calculate compound interest using a simple formula, understand what each part of the formula means, see practical examples, and learn how annual, monthly, quarterly and daily compounding can affect the final amount.

What Is Compound Interest?

Compound interest is interest earned on both the original amount of money and the interest that has already accumulated.

This means that your balance can grow faster because each new interest calculation may include previously earned interest.

Compound interest is commonly used for savings accounts, investments, loans, deposits and other financial products.

Key idea: With compound interest, your interest can earn additional interest over time. The longer the money remains invested or saved, the greater the effect of compounding can become.

Compound Interest Formula

The standard compound interest formula is:

A = P(1 + r/n)nt

Where:

  • A = final amount
  • P = principal or initial amount
  • r = annual interest rate expressed as a decimal
  • n = number of times interest is compounded per year
  • t = number of years

If you want to calculate only the interest earned, subtract the original principal from the final amount:

Compound Interest = A − P

How to Calculate Compound Interest Step by Step

You can calculate compound interest manually by following these steps.

  1. Identify the original principal amount.
  2. Convert the annual interest rate into a decimal.
  3. Determine how many times the interest compounds each year.
  4. Determine the number of years.
  5. Substitute the values into the compound interest formula.
  6. Calculate the final amount.
  7. Subtract the original principal to find the interest earned.

Compound Interest Example

Suppose you deposit $1,000 into an account that earns 5% annual interest, compounded once per year, for 3 years.

Principal (P): $1,000

Annual interest rate: 5% = 0.05

Compounding frequency (n): 1

Time (t): 3 years

Formula: A = P(1 + r/n)nt

A = 1000(1 + 0.05/1)1 × 3

A = 1000(1.05)3

A ≈ $1,157.63

Therefore:

Compound Interest = $1,157.63 − $1,000

Compound Interest ≈ $157.63

Compound Interest With Different Compounding Periods

Interest does not always compound once per year. Financial products may compound interest monthly, quarterly, semi-annually or daily.

Compounding Frequency Times Per Year Value of n
Annually 1 1
Semi-annually 2 2
Quarterly 4 4
Monthly 12 12
Daily 365 365

In the standard formula, the value of n changes depending on how frequently interest is compounded.

How to Calculate Monthly Compound Interest

For monthly compounding, interest is calculated 12 times per year. Therefore, use n = 12.

A = P(1 + r/12)12t

For example, if you invest $5,000 at an annual interest rate of 6% for 5 years with monthly compounding, you would use:

P = $5,000

r = 0.06

n = 12

t = 5

A = 5000(1 + 0.06/12)12 × 5

A ≈ $6,744.25

The approximate interest earned would therefore be:

$6,744.25 − $5,000 = $1,744.25

Compound Interest vs Simple Interest

Simple interest and compound interest both use an interest rate, but they calculate interest differently.

Feature Simple Interest Compound Interest
Interest calculated on Original principal Principal plus accumulated interest
Growth over time Generally linear Can grow faster over time
Previous interest earns interest No Yes
Common use Some loans and basic calculations Savings, investments and many financial products

Why Does Compounding Matter?

Compounding can have a significant effect over long periods because interest earned earlier can become part of the balance used to calculate future interest.

This is sometimes described as earning "interest on interest."

The effect can become more noticeable when you combine a longer time period, regular contributions and a consistent rate of return.

Factors That Affect Compound Interest

Several factors can affect the amount of compound interest you earn or pay.

1. Initial Principal

A larger starting balance generally results in more interest when all other conditions remain the same.

2. Interest Rate

A higher interest rate generally produces a larger final amount, assuming the other variables remain unchanged.

3. Time

The length of time can have a major effect because compounding occurs repeatedly throughout the period.

4. Compounding Frequency

Interest may be compounded annually, quarterly, monthly or daily. Changing the compounding frequency changes the calculation.

Common Mistakes When Calculating Compound Interest

Compound interest calculations can produce incorrect results if the variables are entered incorrectly.

  • Using 5 instead of 0.05 for a 5% interest rate.
  • Forgetting to divide the annual interest rate by the number of compounding periods.
  • Using the wrong number of compounding periods.
  • Confusing the final amount with the interest earned.
  • Forgetting that the formula requires the time period in years.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is interest calculated on the original principal together with interest accumulated during previous periods.

What is the compound interest formula?

The standard formula is A = P(1 + r/n)nt. P is the principal, r is the annual interest rate as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year and t is the number of years.

What is the difference between compound interest and simple interest?

Simple interest is calculated on the original principal, while compound interest is calculated on the principal plus accumulated interest.
